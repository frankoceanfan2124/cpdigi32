Research

I didn't want to just guess at what makes group work function well, that felt a bit lazy for a project literally about group dynamics! So before building this site I read through five sources, a mix of practical workplace advice and one that's actually backed by academic research. Here's what stood out from each, in my own words. Click through if you want to read the full thing, they're all pretty good reads honestly.

1. Where the term "group dynamics" even comes from

This one traces the phrase back to psychologist Kurt Lewin in the 1940s, which I thought was kind of cool actually. He noticed people take on distinct roles the moment they're put in a group together. What I found more useful though was the list of specific behaviours that wreck a group: someone shutting down other people's ideas, a quiet member who just agrees with whoever's loudest, "free riding" where someone lets everyone else carry the work (we've all been in that group). The fix it suggests is mostly about catching these early. Give feedback the moment you notice a problem instead of letting it become the group's normal.

Source: MindTools — "Improving Group Dynamics"

2. It's not just one thing, it's a bunch of factors stacked together

This article splits group dynamics into pieces: who has which role, how people communicate, how decisions actually get made, whether people trust each other. What clicked for me is that these causes compound. A group with unclear goals usually ends up with worse communication too, and that snowballs into people not trusting each other. Its fix is basically sequential. Sort out goals and roles first, because trying to patch communication or trust in a group that still doesn't know what it's for rarely sticks.

Source: Wellhub — "How to Improve Group Dynamics in the Workspace"

3. Groups change shape over time, and that's normal

This was probably the most reassuring source out of the five, honestly it made me feel better about a group project I was in last year! It uses Tuckman's stages (forming, storming, norming, performing, adjourning) to explain that early friction in a group, the "storming" bit, isn't a sign something's broken. It's just a phase most groups go through before they settle. The bit I'll actually use myself: agree early on which channel is for what. Quick questions in chat, actual decisions in a shared doc, that kind of thing, so people aren't missing updates just because they checked the wrong place.

Source: Asana — "The Secret to Great Group Dynamics"

4. Figure out what's actually wrong before trying to fix it

Short but useful point from this one. Don't jump straight to a solution. Actually watch how the group works and talk to people individually first, because the obvious problem is often a symptom of something else. It also calls out weak leadership as one of the most common root causes. When nobody's clearly steering, the most dominant personality in the room tends to fill that gap by default, whether or not they're the right person for it.

Source: DeakinCo. — "6 Strategies for Managing and Improving Team Dynamics"

5. The actual research: Hackman & Wageman's six conditions

This is the one I'd point to if someone asked what this project is actually based on. It's a library guide summarising decades of research by J. Richard Hackman and Ruth Wageman into what separates high-performing teams from mediocre ones. Three conditions they call "Essentials" (a real reason the team needs to exist, the right people on it, everyone agreeing on who's actually a member) account for roughly 60% of a team's success on their own. Three more, the "Enablers" (a workable structure, support from outside the team, access to coaching), only really help once those essentials are already there. That order matters more than I expected. You can't coach your way out of a team that doesn't have a real purpose yet.

Source: Sheridan College Library & Learning Services — "How to Improve Group Dynamics"
